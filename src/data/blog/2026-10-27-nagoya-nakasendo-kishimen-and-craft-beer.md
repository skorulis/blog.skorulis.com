## Day 1: Arrival in Nagoya

On the 27th of October we caught the bus from Nara to Nagoya, arriving at 2:30pm. We stayed at the Prince Smart Inn, our first hotel instead of an Airbnb, but thankfully the room still had plenty of space. It also came with free breakfast, which turned out to be a small sandwich.

I went for another 7km run in the afternoon around Meijo Park. I wasn't quite sure where the running track was, so I started following another runner, but it was a struggle to keep up. It became less of a sightseeing run and more of a race.

We did a load of washing in the hotel, but the dryer just steamed the clothes, so our room ended up with every available space covered in hanging laundry for the next 24 hours. We needed one of the gas dryers that got my clothes dry in 15 minutes.

For dinner we went to Misokatsu Yabaton and got a double serve of crumbed pork with classic and miso sauces. It was tasty, and a lot of pork, for ¥4110 (~$37 AUD) for both of us.

## Day 2: The Nakasendo trail from Magome to Tsumago

On Wednesday we got up at 6am to do a section of the Nakasendo trail. It took two trains, a bus and 2.5 hours to get from Nagoya to the start of the trail, as any accommodation near or along the trail was booked out.

The bus dropped us at Magome, which is a little post town. A post town is a settlement that was built along the highways during the Edo period to provide food, lodging and services to travellers. The ones in this area have retained their historic atmosphere rather than turning into modern towns.

<img src="/images/nagoya/magome-post-town.jpg" alt="The stone-paved main street of Magome post town climbing uphill between traditional dark timber and white plaster buildings, with a hanging wooden souvenir shop sign in the foreground and a big tree under a blue sky at the top of the hill" class="m-0 w-full border border-border" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

Amy stopped at the tourist office to get a certificate, which gets stamped at the start and the end of the walk. The town is tiny and has a single paved street running up the hill to the start of the trail.

From there it's beautiful scenery through moss-covered pine forests. Some of the path is paved, some asphalt and some dirt track. It passes a few guest houses and we stopped at Ichikokutochi tateba teahouse for free hot tea with other hikers. There's also a detour to a pair of waterfalls, a male one and a female one. I didn't know waterfalls had genders. They're called Odaki/Medaki: the larger, stronger one is the male, the smaller gentler one is the female. The water looked lovely, but it was far too cold to be tempted by a swim.

The trail continues on to Tsumago, the next post town on the route. It's a little bigger than Magome with a few restaurants, but we only grabbed an ice cream. Just past the town we sat on a wall in the sun and ate onigiri on the side of the road.

It's possible to stop here and catch the bus, but we kept going to Nagiso to catch the train, which is only another 4km. On that stretch we made a detour to the Tsumago castle ruins. The ruins themselves are nothing more than a few stones, but the path goes through a beautiful bamboo forest and gave us sweeping views over the town while a falcon circled overhead.

We got to Nagiso just as a train was leaving, so we had an hour to wait. That gave us time to walk to Momosuke Bridge, a wooden suspension bridge and easily the nicest thing in Nagiso. There was a park on the other side with some monkey bars, which were a great opportunity to stretch out my spine after weeks of walking with my big backpack. The whole walk was 15km and took 3 hours excluding breaks.

We got a funny little train from Nagiso to Nakatsugawa and then a long train back to Nagoya. It took about 2 hours to get back, so slightly quicker than getting to the start of the trail.

## Day 3: Meijo Park, kishimen and a craft beer crawl

In the morning we walked to Meijo Park past Nagoya Castle. I really like the Espacio hotel next to the castle, which has been built in the same style as a Japanese castle, right down to the large stone fortifications. The park is very spacious with a number of children's play areas, which I didn't climb on today. On the north side of the park is the free Sewerage Science Museum, which teaches kids about sewers and flooding. The best thing there is the outdoor collection of manhole covers from cities all over Japan. You also get a collectible manhole card for visiting.

<img src="/images/nagoya/nagoya-castle-espacio-hotel.jpg" alt="A white turret of Nagoya Castle on its stone walls beside the moat, with the castle-styled Espacio hotel behind it, all reflected in the still water under a cloudy sky" class="m-0 w-full border border-border" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

We tried a few places for lunch. One was located inside the castle grounds, another had a queue and very few tables, so we ended up at Hoshigaoka Seimenjo to try kishimen noodles, a flat udon specific to Nagoya. I found them a little bit slippery, but the red broth was nice and umami-rich. It was only ¥1900 (~$17 AUD) for the two of us and came with some sides, including a tempura seafood stick.

This was near the Chubu Electric Power tower, which looks like a knock-off of the Eiffel Tower, and Oasis 21, which has a shallow pool on the roof that fills the underground level with rippling water reflections. You can also get up onto the top of Oasis 21 for a view over the park. Underneath there is a Studio Ghibli store, which was nice to walk around, but then we walked past Guilty's bakery, which had so much great-looking (and smelling) stuff that we had to get a giant cookie for ¥530 (~$5 AUD). It was a little cake-like, but still very good.

In the afternoon I did another run around Meijo Park, hitting 8.5km this time. It's a really nice city to run in since it's flat and doesn't have much traffic.

My plan was to have dinner in the Fushimi Chikagai underground shopping street, but the places there all looked like somewhere you'd get a beer and a snack rather than a proper dinner. So we went to Brick Lane for burgers and a craft beer, coming to ¥4200 (~$38 AUD). I had a hazy IPA from Totopia Brewing which was so well crafted. From there we walked to Y.Market Brewing, which had a really lively atmosphere of people eating snacks and drinking craft beer. I had two beers here, both of which were great, plus some garlic edamame for ¥3200 (~$29 AUD). Our last stop was Endoji Brewing, which had a huge selection of rare American craft cans at high prices but only a few of their own beers on tap. I tried two here and they were decent, but a step down from the previous two places.

## Day 4: Rainy day museums and the whisky hunt

The next day looked pretty miserable, so we went to the Nagoya City Science Museum. It's got a lot of exhibits, but there's not much English available and I couldn't figure out how to get the audio guide app. It's quite interactive so there's still plenty to do, but it's primarily aimed at children, and there were a lot of school kids running around. There's a very cool tornado generator and some historical planetarium projectors. We skipped the actual planetarium since it was both difficult to get a booking and the 50 minute presentation is only in Japanese.

<img src="/images/nagoya/science-museum-sphere.jpg" alt="The giant silver sphere of the Nagoya City Science Museum planetarium suspended between two glass buildings, with people walking around on the street below" class="m-0 w-full border border-border" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

<img src="/images/nagoya/science-museum-tornado.jpg" alt="A tall swirling column of white mist lit in purple rising several storeys through the atrium of the science museum's tornado lab, with a crowd watching from below and from the balconies above" class="m-0 w-full border border-border" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

The weather had cleared a bit, so we walked to Osu shopping street. I got a beef curry bun as a snack and then lunch at Asakuma Curry. I got a hamburger meal, which is just the patty with rice and cabbage. Only ¥2190 (~$20 AUD), including a beer from a self-pour machine.

After lunch the rain had started again, so we moved on to the samurai museum. This has a large collection of historical armour, swords and guns spread over three floors. There is also a dress-up station at the start for getting a samurai photo. Unlike the science museum, this one was very quiet.

<img src="/images/nagoya/samurai-museum-dress-up.jpg" alt="Me at the samurai museum dress-up station wearing a red kabuto helmet and a black and gold brocade vest, holding a katana in front of a projected backdrop of Nagoya Castle and autumn leaves" class="m-0 w-full border border-border" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

We had a little time left, so we stopped in at the free electricity museum, which is targeted at kids with a bunch of interactive exhibits. I wouldn't recommend it, but it was on the way back to our hotel anyway.

My next goal was to find some nice bottles of whisky. I visited every Liquor Mountain and Bic Camera in the area, but the only place I found with a decent selection was Sakenosokuhai. It didn't do tax free, but it had some really good Japanese options. Before buying anything we went back to a whisky bar in the underground shopping street, where I sampled a Yamazaki 12 for ¥2000 (~$18 AUD), and then returned to the store to make the purchase for ¥25,000 (~$225 AUD). They were charging above retail, but I've never seen any of the good options available at retail prices anyway. It took a lot of walking and searching, but at least I now had something nice to take home. The rain was pretty heavy at this point so we got some dinner and ate in the hotel.

## Day 5: Leaving Nagoya

Overall I really liked Nagoya. It's the first place in Japan we've visited that isn't a tourist hot spot, and it's nice to be somewhere people are just going about their daily lives. The city itself doesn't have any big headline sights, but it's a pleasant city to walk around in and provided a good base to get to the hikes we wanted.

On Saturday the 31st we checked out and walked to Nagoya Station to get the JR Shinano train to Matsumoto, taking about 2 hours. It's a really pretty train ride, winding up into the mountains alongside the river with the trees just starting to change for autumn.
